Build a complete data platform, start to finish

Follow along and you will build a working system that tracks a customer order all the way through to the cash that pays for it — the source database, the data pipeline, the business model and the report. No prior Fabric experience assumed, and nothing to buy.

Why anyone builds this

Order to cash is everything between a customer placing an order and the money landing in the bank. In most companies four different teams own four parts of it.

WhoWhat they can see
SalesThe order was taken
LogisticsThe goods were shipped
FinanceThe invoice was raised
CreditThe payment arrived, eventually

Each lives in a different system with its own definition of the same word, so almost nobody can follow one order all the way through — and the questions worth asking sit precisely in the gaps between those four boxes:

  • Why does one region get paid twenty days later than everywhere else?
  • Is the factory late, or was the delivery promise never achievable?
  • How much of what we call "money owed" is actually collectable?
What you end up with. A system where any one of 16,175 order lines can be traced from order to delivery to invoice to the payment that settled it - 19,578 complete journeys - and where a number on a chart can always be walked back to the transaction that produced it.

What gets built

Seven Fabric components, in four plain moves. Nothing is included just to say it was used.

MoveWhat it doesComponents
1. CopyTake the data out of the source untouched, so reporting never slows the business downSQL Database · Mirroring · Lakehouse (Bronze)
2. CleanTranslate it - dates that were text become dates, codes become namesPySpark notebook (Silver)
3. ModelReshape so one order line sits at the centre and everything else describes itPySpark notebook (Gold) · Dataflow Gen2
4. ServePublish once, so every report and every person uses the same definitionsSemantic model · Ontology · Power BI report
The constraint that shaped the whole design: an ontology can only bind to data through a Direct Lake semantic model. That one fact fixed the storage mode, forced a strict contract on the Gold layer - no Decimal types, no unusual column names - and set the build order for everything downstream.

What it's worth

Three things that only became visible once the whole chain sat in one place.

The same question, two honest answers

Delivery performance is reported twice - against the date the customer asked for (61.3%) and against the date the business confirmed internally (5.4%).

Both are correct. Publishing only the flattering one hides a modelling choice worth fifty percentage points - and it is exactly the number that ends up quoted in a board pack. The report shows both, with the reason written on the page.

2.29m of receivables that do not exist

Adding up euros, dollars and Singapore dollars without converting overstates money owed by 6.3%. The fix isn't clever - it's handling currency once, in one place, rather than separately in every report.

77.7 days from order to cash

Broken into its stages, so the slow step is identified rather than assumed. That decomposition is only possible because the order-line grain survives every hop.

Where this transfers. The furniture manufacturer is invented, but the pattern isn't specific to it - any multi-document process spread across systems has the same problem. Procure-to-pay, loan origination through servicing, meter-to-cash. What transfers is the method: copy faithfully, clean once, agree definitions in one place, and write the caveats where the reader can see them.

What you need before you start

Three things. The second one surprises people.

1. A Microsoft work or school account

The Fabric trial cannot be activated with a personal account — not Gmail, not outlook.com, not hotmail. Microsoft requires a work or school account.

No work account? You can create one. Sign up for the free Microsoft 365 Developer Program, or register a Microsoft 365 Business trial with a domain you control. Either gives you a work-style account (you@yourdomain.com) that Fabric will accept. This is the most common reason people stall on step one.

2. A Fabric trial

Sign in at app.fabric.microsoft.com. Open the account menu in the top right and choose Start trial. You get 60 days of a full Fabric capacity at no cost, which is more than enough for this build.

The trial expires, and when it does the workspace becomes unreachable. Everything inside it goes — notebooks, tables, reports. Note your expiry date now and set a reminder for three days before. Step 9 is the export, and it is not optional.

3. Power BI Desktop

Free, Windows only, from Microsoft's site. Used only at the last step.

Do I need to write code?

No. The code is provided and commented. You import it, attach it to your data, and press Run. You will read error messages and click through settings screens — but you will not be writing Python or SQL from scratch. If you want to understand what the code does, every step has a collapsible For the technical reader section.

1

Set up your workspace

about 20 min

Everything you build lives in one Fabric workspace — think of it as a project folder in the cloud that holds databases, code and reports side by side.

  1. Sign in at app.fabric.microsoft.com with your work account.
  2. Workspaces → New workspace. Name it something like Nordhaus-O2C-Dev.
  3. Under Advanced, set the licence mode to your Trial capacity. This is the step people miss — a workspace on the wrong capacity cannot create most Fabric items.
  4. Write your trial expiry date somewhere you will see it.
Set a calendar reminder for three days before the trial ends. When the trial lapses the workspace stops working and everything inside it becomes unreachable — notebooks, data, reports. Export before then, not after. This is the single most common way people lose a build like this.
2

Create the pretend SAP system

about 2–3 h

A real project starts with data already sitting in a source system. You don't have one, so you build a convincing stand-in: a database shaped exactly like SAP, filled with invented but realistic orders.

Why bother making it look like SAP? Because the hard part of a real project is never the charts — it's the source system's quirks. SAP stores dates as text, pads customer numbers with zeros, and splits paid and unpaid invoices across two different tables. If your practice data is tidy, you practise the easy version and learn nothing.
  1. In your workspace: New item → SQL database. Name it SAP_S4H_Source.
  2. Open the query editor and run 01_SAP_Source_DDL.sql from the download. This creates about 25 empty tables with genuine SAP names — VBAK for order headers, VBAP for order lines, BSID for unpaid invoices, and so on.
  3. Import 04_Nordhaus_Generate_SAP_Source.ipynb and run it. It invents two years of trading — customers, products, orders, deliveries, invoices and payments — and loads them in.
What is a notebook? A document that mixes explanation with runnable code. You click Run and it executes top to bottom. You do not need to write any of it — the notebooks are provided and commented. Reading them is optional; running them is not.
For the technical reader — what the generator actually does
Deliberate process pathology is built in, because a flat demo teaches nothing: ~8% of orders are credit-blocked and concentrated in one customer segment, ~12% of deliveries split, ~5% are partial, there is a Q4 demand spike with matching OTIF degradation, and one customer cohort pays structurally late. Volume is controlled by a single SCALE = 0.10 constant. Dates are written as CHAR(8) DATS strings where empty is '00000000', and keys carry ALPHA leading zeros.
3

Bring the data into the lakehouse

about 1–2 h

Next you copy the data out of the pretend SAP system into a lakehouse — a storage area that can hold huge tables cheaply and let you query them like a database.

Why not just report on the SAP database directly? Two reasons. Reporting queries would slow the operational system down, and you want a permanent history even if the source changes. Every serious data platform copies first, transforms second.
  1. New item → Lakehouse. Name it OntologyDataLH.
  2. Turn on mirroring on the SQL database. Fabric then copies every table into the lakehouse automatically and keeps them in step — no code required. This copy is your Bronze layer: raw, untouched, exactly as it came.
  3. Add a Dataflow Gen2 for the bits that never lived in SAP — exchange rates and monthly sales targets. This is a point-and-click tool; no code.
The file format dropdown quietly defaults to Binary
When you set up a copy step, the File format field starts as Binary and selecting Parquet can fail silently if the dropdown scrolls as you click. You get “Destination must be binary when source is of binary format.” The reliable tell: the Mapping tab only becomes clickable once Parquet is genuinely selected.
4

Clean the data up

about 3–4 h

Now the translation work. SAP's conventions get turned into something a normal person can read, and the results are saved as a new set of tables called Silver.

What actually changes here. Text like 20260131 becomes a real date. 0000012345 becomes 12345. The two separate invoice tables — one for unpaid, one for paid — become a single list with a paid/unpaid flag. And cryptic column names become CustomerName and OrderDate.
  1. Import 06_Bronze_to_Silver.ipynb.
  2. Attach your lakehouse to it (see the trap below — this one catches almost everyone).
  3. Run all. It produces 18 cleaned tables and prints a set of checks at the end.
“No default context found” — you attached the wrong thing
A lakehouse appears twice in the attach list under the same name: once as the lakehouse, once as its SQL endpoint. Pick the wrong one and the notebook fails with UnsupportedOperationException: No default context found, even though the panel looks correct. The tell: the wrong one has no “pin as default” in its menu. Remove it, re-add the row with the lighter-blue lakehouse icon, accept Stop current session, and check a small pin appears next to the name. This cost time on three separate occasions during the original build.
The notebook won't import — 400 Bad Request
If you edit a notebook outside Fabric and re-upload it, the import can fail with a useless message. Fabric needs the file's internal text stored as a list of lines and pure ASCII throughout. Use the notebooks as downloaded and this never arises.
5

Build the business model

about 3–4 h

Silver is clean but still shaped like a source system. Gold reshapes it into the form reporting tools expect: one central table of order lines, surrounded by lookup tables for customer, product, plant, date and so on.

The one number that proves it worked. The finished central table has exactly 16,175 rows — the same as the original SAP order-line table. If a join is wrong, this number inflates. It is the cheapest possible check that nothing was accidentally duplicated along the way.
  1. Import and run 07_Silver_to_Gold.ipynb.
  2. Read the checks it prints at the end. It should finish with ALL CHECKS PASSED.
  3. Optionally run 08_Gold_Document_Lineage.ipynb to add the tables that trace each order line all the way through to the payment that settled it.
A validation that runs after the save is not a validation
In these notebooks the tables are written before the checks run, so data lands whether or not the checks pass. On the original build the output was never read and two checks were quietly failing. Read the final cell's output. It takes ten seconds.
For the technical reader — the Gold contract
Before any write, a gate rejects any DecimalType column (Fabric Graph returns null for Decimal) and any column name not matching ^[A-Za-z][A-Za-z0-9_]*$ (anything else triggers Delta column mapping). Money is rounded to 2dp after the cast to double, or IEEE-754 artefacts like 5189.200082400001 surface later in the ontology browser. Customer and material are SCD2; the fact is an accumulating snapshot at order-line grain.
6

Create the reporting model

about 2 h

A semantic model is the layer that turns tables into business language. It defines how tables relate, and holds the calculations — "OTIF %", "Open receivables" — so every report uses the same definition instead of each analyst inventing their own.

  1. From the lakehouse, choose New semantic model and pick the 13 Gold tables.
  2. Check the relationships Fabric guessed, and correct them.
  3. Add the calculations. Paste the provided script into DAX query view, click Run, then Update model with changes — this adds all 24 at once rather than typing them one at a time.
  4. Set number formats: percentages as Percentage with 1 decimal, otherwise they display as 0.58 instead of 58.2%.
“Select all” ignores the search box
Filter the table list to find your 13 tables, click Select all, and you get all 36 — the filter is visual only. Untick Select all, then tick the tables individually.
A new relationship says it “needs to be recalculated”
This is not an error, and it confirms the relationship was created. Refresh the semantic model and the message disappears.
7

Add the ontology

about 1–2 h

An ontology describes your business as things and relationships — a Customer places an Order, which ships from a Plant — rather than as tables and joins. It is the newest layer in Fabric and the most interesting one to show people.

  1. From the semantic model, choose Generate ontology.
  2. Open each entity type and set its key. Nothing works until every entity has one.
  3. Rename the relationships to business language. Fabric generates names like gld_fact_order_to_cash_has_gld_dim_customer; soldTo reads far better.
What works well. The ontology binds live to your Gold tables, so new data appears without rebuilding anything, and relationships you already defined in the semantic model come across automatically.
Fabric Graph is not ready — plan around it
Two separate problems were found. First, ontology relationships do not become graph edges — edges are a different thing that must be built from a source table, and nothing tells you, so the graph just shows disconnected dots. Second, edges created by hand disappeared after closing and reopening the item. The graph layer was abandoned on the original build. The ontology itself is unaffected and works properly.
8

Build the report

about 3–4 h

Finally, the part everyone recognises. Connect Power BI to the published semantic model and build the pages.

  1. Power BI Desktop → Get data → Power BI semantic models → pick yours. This is a live connection: the data stays in Fabric.
  2. Build five pages: a landing page, an executive summary, delivery performance, cash and receivables, and a guide page explaining how to read the report.
  3. Publish back to the workspace.
The one design decision worth copying. Report on-time delivery twice — against the date the customer asked for (61.3%) and the date the business confirmed internally (5.4%). Both are true. Showing one alone hides a choice worth fifty percentage points, and it is exactly the number someone will quote in a meeting.
Never add up a column of mixed currencies
The receivables are in euros, dollars and Singapore dollars. Adding them gives 38.6m against a true 36.4m — a 6.3% overstatement of money owed. Split by company code, and switch the table's Totals row off so the meaningless number cannot be read by accident.
9

Export everything before the trial ends

about 1 h

The least interesting step and the one that matters most. When the trial expires the workspace becomes unreachable, and only what you saved to your own computer survives.

  1. Screenshot every report page at full size.
  2. Screenshot the workspace item list, the dataflow editor, the model diagram and the ontology.
  3. Copy the Dataflow's M code out of the Advanced Editor into a text file — it exists nowhere else.
  4. Script the semantic model out of TMDL view and save it.
  5. Export the Gold tables to Parquet or CSV.
  6. Download the .pbix.
On the original build this was discovered with eight days left and no export done. Do it the week you finish, not the week it expires.

What this build does not do

Stated plainly, so nothing here oversells itself.

  • The data is invented. Nordhaus Group is fictional. The shape is realistic; no figure represents a real company.
  • The pipeline is partial. One working copy step, not the full automated framework.
  • Fabric Graph was abandoned. Edges did not survive being reopened.
  • Five report pages of seven planned, and 24 calculations of roughly 70.
  • Trial capacity only. Nothing here says what this would cost to run in production.
If you build this and get further than I did — particularly on the pipeline or the graph — I'd genuinely like to hear about it.
